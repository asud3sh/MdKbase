# Prerequisites & Mindset

## 1.1 Who Is This Course For?

This course assumes that you:

* Have little or no prior knowledge of Russian.
* May not be familiar with the Cyrillic alphabet.
* Want to understand and use Russian in practical situations.
* Are willing to practice consistently rather than study intensively for a few days and stop.
* Want to understand how Russian works, not merely memorize translated phrases.

No prior knowledge of Russian grammar is required.

Knowledge of English can help with understanding grammatical explanations, but English and Russian differ substantially in pronunciation, grammar, and sentence structure.

## 1.2 What Does A1 Mean?

A1 is the first level of the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR).

At A1, a learner can generally:

* Understand and use familiar everyday expressions.
* Introduce themselves and other people.
* Ask and answer simple questions about personal information.
* Interact in simple ways when the other person speaks slowly and clearly.
* Read and understand short, simple texts.
* Write basic sentences about themselves and familiar topics.

A1 does not mean fluency.

The goal of this course is to build a reliable beginner foundation, not to create the illusion of fluency through memorized dialogues.

## 1.3 The Five Principles of Learning Russian

### Principle 1 — Cyrillic Is a Skill, Not a Memorization Test

Do not treat Russian letters as mysterious symbols that must be translated into English.

Learn to recognize each letter, associate it with its sound, and read it inside real Russian words.

For example:

* мама — mama
* дом — house
* кот — cat
* метро — metro

Initially, transliteration may help. However, it should gradually disappear.

**Target:** By the end of Week 1, read familiar Russian words directly from Cyrillic without relying on English-letter approximations.

### Principle 2 — Pronunciation Begins on Day 1

Russian spelling is relatively systematic, but written vowels do not always sound the same in unstressed positions.

For example:

молоко́ — milk

The written vowels are о, о, о, but the unstressed vowels are reduced in ordinary pronunciation.

You will therefore learn four things together:

1. Letter recognition.
2. Sound production.
3. Word stress.
4. Listening recognition.

Never assume that correctly identifying the letters automatically means you are pronouncing a word correctly.

### Principle 3 — Learn Complete Expressions

Instead of learning only:

* привет — hi
* как — how
* дела — affairs/things

Learn:

**Привет! Как дела?**
Hi! How are you?

This gives you a usable expression rather than three disconnected vocabulary items.

As the course progresses, you will gradually learn to modify these expressions and create your own sentences.

### Principle 4 — Recall Before Reviewing

When reviewing, first attempt to remember the answer without looking.

For example:

1. Look at the English meaning: "Thank you."
2. Recall the Russian expression.
3. Say it aloud.
4. Write it from memory.
5. Check the answer.

This is more useful than repeatedly rereading a vocabulary list.

### Principle 5 — Accuracy Before Speed, Then Gradually Build Speed

At first, slow and accurate pronunciation is preferable to fast, inaccurate pronunciation.

However, do not remain dependent on slow, deliberate translation forever.

As your familiarity increases, practice responding to simple questions without mentally translating every word.

# 2. Daily Study Template: 60–90 Minutes

Use this template for most study days.

| Activity                |  Duration | Purpose                                   |
| ----------------------- | --------: | ----------------------------------------- |
| Previous lesson review  |    10 min | Retrieve older material from memory       |
| Pronunciation/listening | 10–15 min | Improve sound recognition and production  |
| New lesson              | 15–20 min | Learn the day's content                   |
| Guided exercises        | 10–15 min | Apply the new material accurately         |
| Speaking/writing        | 10–15 min | Produce Russian independently             |
| End-of-session recall   |  5–10 min | Identify what you can and cannot remember |

For a 60-minute session, shorten the new lesson or practice section.

For a 90-minute session, spend the extra time on listening, speaking, or correcting mistakes.

### The most important rule

Do not measure learning by the number of minutes spent looking at Russian.

Measure it by what you can recognize, understand, recall, and produce without assistance.

# 3. Weekly Rhythm: 6 Study Days + 1 Review/Rest Day

Each week contains six progressive study days and one consolidation day.

| Day   | Learning mode                           |
| ----- | --------------------------------------- |
| Day 1 | Introduce foundational concepts         |
| Day 2 | Expand recognition and understanding    |
| Day 3 | Build combinations and patterns         |
| Day 4 | Apply knowledge to practical situations |
| Day 5 | Increase independent production         |
| Day 6 | Integrate the week's material           |
| Day 7 | Review, self-test, or rest              |

Day 7 is not necessarily a full day off.

If you feel mentally fatigued, rest. If you feel ready, perform a short review without introducing new material.

# 5. Pronunciation & Stress Key

Russian pronunciation will be introduced gradually, beginning with the alphabet.

The following notation will be used throughout the course.

| Notation       | Meaning                                                                       |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| а́              | Stressed а                                                                    |
| о́              | Stressed о                                                                    |
| е́              | Stressed е                                                                    |
| IPA            | International Phonetic Alphabet                                               |
| Hard consonant | Consonant without the Russian palatalized articulation                        |
| Soft consonant | Palatalized consonant, produced with the tongue raised toward the hard palate |

## 5.1 Stress

Russian words have lexical stress.

Stress determines which syllable receives prominence and often affects vowel pronunciation.

For example:

* ма́ма — mother
* окно́ — window
* молоко́ — milk

Stress marks will be shown in instructional vocabulary whenever useful.

In ordinary Russian writing, stress marks are generally omitted, except in special educational or disambiguating contexts.

## 5.2 Transliteration

Transliteration is the representation of Russian letters using Latin letters.

It may be used temporarily to support beginners, but it is not a substitute for learning Russian pronunciation.

For example:

Привет! — privet — Hi!

The Latin representation is only an approximation. Russian sounds do not always correspond directly to English sounds.

**Course convention:** Cyrillic comes first. Transliteration is supporting information, not the main reading system.

# 6. Progress Tracking & Self-Testing

Track progress according to demonstrated ability, not merely completed lessons.

## 6.1 Weekly Progress Record

At the end of each week, record:

| Category   | Self-assessment                                         |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reading    | Can I read the week's material without transliteration? |
| Listening  | Can I recognize familiar words when spoken?             |
| Speaking   | Can I produce the week's phrases without reading?       |
| Writing    | Can I write familiar words and sentences from memory?   |
| Grammar    | Can I explain and apply the week's main pattern?        |
| Vocabulary | Can I recall the week's core words in context?          |

Use the following ratings:

* **0 — Not yet learned**
* **1 — Recognize with help**
* **2 — Recall with effort**
* **3 — Use independently**

## 6.2 Weekly Advancement Rule

Aim for at least 80% on the weekly mini-test, while also being able to complete the core speaking task.

If you score below 80%, review the relevant lessons before advancing.

Do not restart the entire week automatically. Identify the specific gaps and practice those.

## 6.3 The Error Notebook

Maintain a simple record of recurring mistakes.

| Date | Incorrect form | Correct form | Reason |
| ---- | -------------- | ------------ | ------ |
|      |                |              |        |

Examples of errors worth recording:

* Confusing Н and H.
* Misplacing stress.
* Forgetting a greeting's appropriate context.
* Using an incorrect gender form.
* Mispronouncing a soft consonant.

The purpose is not to record every minor mistake. It is to identify patterns that need correction.
